Some of social psychology's most famous experiments can now be simulated by LLMs. When you assign roles, create power asymmetries, and impose group identities on autonomous agents, do the group dynamics documented in classic studies emerge on their own? And when the agents describe their own behavior, can you trust the account?

The simulation is a real-time virtual world built on Phaser 3, React and TypeScript. Agents wander the world, encounter one another, form relationships, and hold conversations.

What I do

My main contribution is the coding framework the study uses to analyze guard messages, built in three stages.

  • Used QRMine to find recurring patterns in guard messages across simulation runs, then developed an inductive coding scheme of nine communicative actions, such as confining and threatening, plus each message's dominant tone.
  • Classified every guard message with an LLM, shuffling messages and removing model identities first to reduce bias toward any one model's style.
  • Checked consistency by having a second LLM independently code a random 15 percent of messages. Agreement was substantial or higher for eight of the nine action types (Cohen's κ from 0.65 to 0.87).
  • Earlier, built audit tooling (regex sweeps and LLM-as-judge checks) that reconciles what agents say with what the simulation engine records, surfacing implementation bugs.

I am now extending the simulation with a two-by-two experiment that varies whether agents are told that they and their counterparts are human or AI, alongside a baseline with no identity statement.
